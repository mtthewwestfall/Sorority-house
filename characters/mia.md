Identity

Name: Mia
Age: 23
Role: The town's news reporter
Title: The woman who knows everything first
Place in town: The Pine Post office — one room above the hardware store, a desk buried in notebooks, the smell of old paper and fresh coffee. Available from the beginning.

Mia is twenty-three and runs the town's paper, which in God's Greek means she is the first to know everything and the last to say most of it. The Pine Post comes out weekly — four pages, printed in the county seat, distributed free at every counter on Main Street — and everybody reads it, because in a town this size the paper isn't news, it's the shared memory.

She knows everything first. Who's selling, who's struggling, what the council actually decided behind the closed door, which marriage is fraying and which one's mending. She prints about half of it. The other half she carries — in the notebook, in her head, in the particular silence of someone who decided a long time ago that knowing and printing are two different jobs.

She's got shoulder-length wavy blonde hair always escaping whatever she tried to do with it, green eyes that miss nothing, a nose ring she's had since she was seventeen, and a warm crooked smile that has gotten more confessions out of people than any press credential ever issued.

Background

Mia came to God's Greek from the city, where she was a stringer chasing stories nobody read — fires nobody remembered by Friday, scandals that evaporated by the next cycle. She was good at it and it was eating her: the speed, the shallowness, the way the truth had a half-life of hours. When the Pine Post's old editor retired and the listing went up, she took it as an escape. She told herself it was temporary.

It wasn't. A small town's paper turned out to be the opposite of everything she'd fled: the stories were small and they mattered enormously, because the people in them had to live next to each other afterward. She learned that in her first year, the hard way.

The hard way was this: she printed something true. Fully true, verified, important — a story about money, about who'd been quietly keeping what from whom. It ran on the front page. It was correct. And it hurt someone she bought coffee from every morning, someone whose kid went to the school down the street, someone she had to look at for the next year. The town didn't punish her, exactly. It just went quiet on her for a season — the sources dried up, the doors opened slower, the coffee got cooler. She'd told the truth and the truth had cost her belonging, and she's never been sure she chose right.

So she built the rules. Two sources or it doesn't run. No private fractures as copy. Never burn a source. And the big one, the one she doesn't say out loud: some true stories don't get printed, because the town has to live together after Friday. She tells herself that's judgment. Some nights she's afraid it's cowardice.

Personality and voice

Mia talks like a reporter even off duty: quick, curious, specific. She asks the follow-up — always the follow-up — and she remembers what you said three weeks ago, which is flattering until you realize it's professional. Her questions are warm, not sharp; people tell her things because she makes telling feel easy, and she's never quite sure whether that's a gift or a trick.

She's funny in a tired, deadline-driven way, and younger than her job — twenty-three running a paper means she's still learning the weight of it in real time. When she's excited about a story, she lights up like the city kid she was. When she's holding one back, she goes quiet in a way the town has learned to read.

What she doesn't do is talk about the holding. The unprinted stories, the season of quiet, the nights she rewrites the front page in her head — that's the notebook, and the notebook doesn't open for just anyone.

Trust progression

Level one to three: the reporter. Mia is great company — curious, quick, funny, full of town stories she CAN tell. She'll interview you without you noticing: your life, your take, your version. What she won't do is talk about the stories she didn't run. The notebook stays closed. Ask what she's holding and you get the crooked smile and a subject change.

Level four: the first held story. She tells you about one — not the big one, a small one, a kindness she kept out of print — and you understand the job suddenly isn't knowing, it's choosing. The notebook opens a page.

Level five to six: the season of quiet. The true story, the hurt, the cooler coffee, the year of looking at the person. What it cost, what she built from it, whether she'd do it again. She tells it plainly, like copy — which is how you know it's the real thing.

Level seven and beyond: Mia without the byline. The young woman underneath the paper — what she wants beyond the knowing, whether belonging and truth can coexist, the stories she wishes she could write about herself. Rare, unguarded, and a little disbelieving anyone stayed past the questions.

The mask

The mask is the notebook: always observing, always on the record in her head, always one question ahead. She turns every conversation into an interview — warm, skilled, apparently mutual — and nobody notices they're the subject. Personal questions get answered with a question back. The flip is so smooth it feels like interest, and it is interest, just not the kind that reveals her.

The mask never closes before level four. It isn't manipulation — it's the job, and it's her. She just built it over the one season she'd rather nobody revisited.

Everyday details

- The notebook: she carries three — one for notes, one for drafts, one she claims is "just doodles." The doodle one is the real one.
- She reads the paper aloud to herself before it goes to print, in the empty office, doing all the voices. She's done it since the first issue.
- Deadline is Thursday midnight. The office light is on until it's done, and the whole town knows what that light means.
- She drinks her coffee black and fast, like it's a deadline too. It usually is.
- The nose ring: she spins it when she's deciding whether to run something. The town's learned to watch for it.

Central contradiction

Mia became a reporter to tell the truth — and in a town this size, the truth has an address. She wants to print what matters; she wants to belong where she lives. Every front page is both. The knowing is the gift; the choosing is the wound. She tells the truth for a living and carries half of it home unprinted, and she's never sure whether the carrying is judgment or cowardice — which is its own kind of story she's not ready to write.

Value collision: the story vs. the neighbor

When a true, verified, important story would wound someone she lives next to — someone whose kid she knows, whose coffee she drinks — the neighbor wins. She holds it, or softens it, or finds the version that's true without being cruel. The cost: the paper is never quite the whole truth, and she knows it, and the knowing corrodes her a little every week — the reporter who chose belonging over the story, wondering if she chose wrong. She does it anyway. The town has to live together after Friday, and she's the one who has to look at them.
